The open force-on-force platform

Atalaia is an open platform for the electronic props of airsoft, paintball and milsim: the boxes teams capture, the bombs they arm and defuse. Build a domination box from a €20 microcontroller (a tiny, cheap computer board), turn an old Android phone into a hacking terminal, run a 20-box weekend milsim on a LoRa mesh (long-range, low-power radio that the boxes relay to one another across the field), and write your own game modes as small scripts. Every game runs on the field itself: no cloud or internet needed, and no manufacturer can lock you in.

Atalaia (ah-tah-LYE-ah) is Portuguese for watchtower: a lookout on a hill that watched for trouble and passed the warning along. Why that name?

How a game works

Playing needs no technical knowledge. This is how a game will run once the boxes are built (phases 1 and 2); the simulator already plays the same modes on a computer today.

  1. Set up

    The organisers place the boxes around the field and pick a game mode, such as Domination, on a box's keypad or from a phone, in under a minute.

  2. Capture

    To take a box, a team holds its colour's button for a few seconds. The box lights up in that colour and scores for the team until the other side takes it back.

  3. Relay

    Each box reports by radio to the field controller, a small computer at the base; boxes far away are relayed by the ones in between. A box that loses contact keeps playing and catches up later.

  4. Score

    The controller adds it all up. The organisers follow the game live on their phones, and the field can show the score on a big screen or on a stream.

Why Atalaia

Electronic props add a lot to a game, but commercial systems keep the rules locked inside closed firmware. New modes wait on the vendor, there is no public API, and you cannot add your own hardware. Atalaia does it differently.

  • Local-first

    Every game runs on the field itself, fully offline, from a single house to a multi-kilometre forest. The internet is optional.

  • Any hardware

    Cheap ESP32 boards, credit-card-sized Raspberry Pi computers, simple Arduino-class boards and new or repurposed Android phones all play in the same game.

  • Modes are scripts

    Game modes are short Lua scripts, small text files of rules, with a settings file. Change or share one without reflashing (reinstalling) the boxes' built-in software.

  • Open protocol and API

    Props talk over Skirmesh-RF, a documented radio protocol, so anyone can build a device that joins in. The field controller offers MQTT, REST and WebSocket APIs for scoreboards, stream overlays and other tools.

  • Built for fields

    Player roles, squads, custom scenarios, and a chrono registry that keeps each replica's readings for spot checks are part of the design.

  • Copyleft

    Fields that charge entry and makers who sell boxes are welcome. Anyone who ships or hosts a changed version shares the changes back.

How it fits together

In plain words: the props on the field talk by radio to the field controller, a small computer at the base that runs the game and keeps the score. Phones connect to it over Wi-Fi, and scoreboards, stream overlays and map apps such as ATAK through its API. An optional site server on the internet keeps accounts and history. The controller, its API and the organisers' console are built and tested on a computer, not yet on the field; the site server comes in a later phase.

Under the hood, every device that runs game modes uses the same shared core, written in C11, and the same Lua modes; only a thin layer for each kind of hardware differs. A mode written once plays the same on a box, a phone or a Raspberry Pi.

How the radio works

The props talk over LoRa, a long-range, low-power radio, in Atalaia's own protocol, Skirmesh-RF. These two diagrams show what LoRa can and cannot do on a field, and how one protocol runs on very different devices. None of it has been on a real field yet: range and timing will be measured in the phase 2 field test.

Which channel, and why 869.525 MHz

All of a game's traffic shares one channel. The default, 869.525 MHz, is the centre of 869.4–869.65 MHz, the only part of the European 868 MHz band where a device may transmit up to 10% of the time at up to 500 mW. Elsewhere in 868–870 MHz the rules allow 1% of the time (0.1% on 868.7–869.2 MHz) at 25 mW: about a tenth of the airtime, and much less range. 868.1, 868.3 and 868.5 MHz are LoRaWAN's default channels and often busy. The default is shared too: LoRaWAN gateways send some replies on 869.525 MHz and Meshtastic's European default uses it, so near such users the GM can let the controller listen to every legal channel for a minute and pick the quietest, draw one at random, or choose by hand. The boxes then follow the chosen channel's own limits.

Game modes

Modes react to intent ("a team is claiming this prop"), not to hardware, so the same script runs on a box with two arcade buttons, a keypad box or a tablet. Two classics are written and tested today.

Domination

A team holds the prop's input for a few seconds to claim it, and scores every tick while it keeps it. The other team can take it over at any time.

Hold to capture · scores per tick · any number of zones

Bomb / Rush

Attackers hold to arm the bomb, then defend it. Defenders must hold long enough to defuse it before the fuse runs out. Fuse and hold times are settings.

Arm · defend · defuse or explode

Try it today

There is nothing to flash yet, but the shared core runs on a computer. The simulator plays a mode in your browser: hold team buttons, watch each prop's screen and follow the event log. The commands below are typed in a terminal.

With a C compiler

cmake -S core -B build -DATALAIA_SHARED=ON
cmake --build build
cd simulator
python -m atalaia_sim serve ../modes/bomb

With Docker only

cd simulator
python ../tools/docker_sim.py serve ../modes/bomb

Then open http://localhost:8000. You need Python 3.10+ with PyYAML. The simulator guide has every option.

Roadmap

Six phases. Each one ends with a real field test before the next begins.

  1. Done

    0 · Foundations

    Shared core, Lua sandbox, Skirmesh-RF codec, browser simulator.

    Gate: the simulator runs Domination and Bomb from Lua scripts. Met.

  2. Software done

    1 · Single box

    Field Box build, ESP32 firmware, on-device presets, and a build manual with a wiring diagram.

    Gate: one box runs a 2-hour club game with no restarts. The firmware and its screens are done and run in an emulator, and the build manual with its wiring diagrams is written; the gate stays open until the first boxes are built.

  3. In progress

    2 · Field network

    Skirmesh-RF over LoRa, field controller, API, GM console, and a mode pack, now written and tested in software: Hold-All Domination (score only while holding every box), Last Stand (a countdown to defeat while holding one of three), per-team arming codes on Bomb, and the Codes modifier (keypad codes that grant points, hints or unlocks, with the GM told who used them).

    Gate: 5 boxes on a CQB site and an outdoor field, zero lost captures. The encrypted radio protocol, the field controller with its API, the USB radio modem, the organisers' console with a scoreboard, joining a game with an NFC card or a QR code, and the boxes' firmware talking to the controller are built and tested in the simulator, the emulator and on a computer, whole games included. The gate needs the radio boards.

  4. 3 · Android and players

    Android props, I/O pod, player app with roles, and the modes that need players' phones: Respawn, Kill Confirmed, Player Check-in, Money Factory and chrono check-in. Started: Kill Confirmed, Respawn and Lives and Player Check-in read players' signed NFC tags on the Field Box, the I/O pod's link protocol is fixed, and modes describe their screens in YAML; all tested in software, no Android code yet.

    Gate: one game mixes ESP32 and Android props without issues.

  5. 4 · Site server and advanced play

    Site server, custom scenarios, advanced modes, ATAK.

    Gate: a weekend milsim runs on Atalaia end to end.

  6. 5 · Community

    Visual mode editor, mode registry, carrier PCB (a ready-made circuit board that makes builds easier), translations, build guides for other node types.

    Ongoing after the first release.

Glossary

The technical words on this page, in plain terms. The links in the text above lead here.

The game

Props
The electronic devices of a game: boxes to capture, bombs to arm and defuse, terminals to hack, targets to hit. In Atalaia any device can be one: a purpose-built box, a phone or a small board.
Field Box and Satellite
Atalaia's own prop designs, built from off-the-shelf parts around an ESP32. The Field Box is the main one, with big arcade buttons, a keypad, a screen, lights, a speaker and an NFC reader in a weatherproof case, for roughly €70–110 in parts; the Satellite is a smaller box for a secondary point, for about €30–40. The first Field Box prototypes come in phase 1.
Milsim
Short for military simulation: long games, often over a whole weekend, with missions, a chain of command and realistic rules. It is the biggest test for Atalaia: 10 to 20 boxes and satellites spread over a large area.
CQB
Close-quarters battle: games inside houses, palacetes, warehouses and other tight spaces, usually under about 150 m across. Everything is close and fast, so Atalaia's CQB profile uses shorter capture and bomb times, and places the boxes on a floor plan instead of using GPS.
Chrono and joules
A chrono (chronograph) measures how fast a replica fires a BB. From the speed and the BB's weight comes its energy in joules, which fields cap per type of replica: a 0.20 g BB at 100 m/s (about 328 fps) carries 1 joule. Atalaia will keep each replica's readings, so marshals can run spot checks during the day; a person always decides any penalty.

Hardware

Microcontroller
A complete, tiny computer on a single chip, made to run one program. It starts in a moment, uses very little battery and is cheap, which makes it the usual brain of an electronic prop. ESP32 and Arduino boards are microcontroller boards.
ESP32
A popular, cheap microcontroller with Wi-Fi and Bluetooth built in; many boards also carry a LoRa radio. It is the brain of the Field Box, the Satellite and the I/O pod, and keeps a prop going for one to three days on a battery. Boards people already own can join too, even without extra memory (PSRAM), with a few limits.
Arduino-class board, thin node
Simple, very cheap microcontroller boards in the Arduino style, such as those with AVR or RP2040 chips. They are too small to run game modes, so in Atalaia they act as thin nodes: they lend their buttons, lights or sensors to a nearby box or to the field controller, which runs the rules for them.
Raspberry Pi
A small, cheap computer about the size of a credit card, running Linux. With a LoRa radio board added, it will be the usual field controller (about €80–150 for the whole build), and it can also run props that need a big screen or a camera.
Android prop and I/O pod
The Atalaia app will turn an Android phone or tablet, new or a spare one from 2017 or later, into a prop: a hacking terminal, a map, a code scanner. Phones have no LoRa radio and touch screens are hard to use with gloves, so a small ESP32 box, the I/O (input/output) pod, will connect by cable or Bluetooth to add the radio, big buttons, lights and a siren. Both arrive in phase 3.
Firmware and flashing
Firmware is the program built into a device, which starts when you switch it on. Flashing means writing a new version of it onto the device, usually over a USB cable. Atalaia boxes are flashed by cable once; after that, new game modes arrive as scripts, and firmware updates come from the field controller over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth, undoing themselves if a new version fails to start.

Radio and connections

LoRa
Short for long range: a radio that sends small messages far on very little power, with no SIM card, mobile coverage or subscription. On a typical field it reaches roughly 0.5–2 km, and gets through thick walls better than Wi-Fi. The catch: messages must be short, and each device may transmit only a small share of the time. Atalaia uses 869.525 MHz by default, in the only EU868 sub-band that allows a 10% duty cycle and 500 mW, giving each box up to 6 minutes of airtime per hour — enough for events, scores and time-sync beacons with headroom to spare. A field can choose another channel, which allows 1% of the time or less and 25 mW. Boxes send only what changes, such as “red captured B”.
Mesh
A network in which devices pass messages on for each other, so a box too far from the base is heard through the boxes in between. Atalaia repeats each message only a couple of times, so the radio does not clog, and organisers can place extra boxes as repeaters on difficult ground.
Skirmesh-RF
Atalaia's own radio protocol: the agreed format in which boxes, the controller and apps exchange messages, over LoRa, Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. It is documented in public, so anyone can build a device that joins in, and every message is signed with a key for that game, so a player with a radio cannot simply fake a capture. The code that packs and checks these messages, the codec, exists today.
Wi-Fi, BLE, NFC and QR codes
Everyday short-range links, each with its own job. Wi-Fi (about 50–150 m) carries the bulky things: the organisers' console, the player app, new modes and updates; BLE, or Bluetooth Low Energy (10–30 m), links a phone to its pod and pairs devices. NFC is tap-to-read, like a contactless card, for team tags, wristbands and join cards, and QR codes are scanned with a phone camera to join a game or enter a code.
API: MQTT, REST and WebSocket
An API is a documented way for other programs to read a game and, with permission, control it. With it a club will be able to drive a big LED scoreboard, a live score overlay on a stream, Discord or Telegram alerts, or a relay that sets off smoke and sirens when the bomb goes off. MQTT, REST and WebSocket are three standard ways in, so ordinary tools work with it. The field controller has it since phase 2, with a ready-made scoreboard page; it has been tested on a computer and not yet on a field.
ATAK
The Android Team Awareness Kit: a map app made for the military, with a free civilian version that many milsim teams use to see their team mates on a shared map. Atalaia's field controller will be able to show the boxes on those maps, and which team holds each one (phase 4).

Software

Local-first
Everything a game needs runs on the field itself, on the boxes and the field controller, so it works with no internet or mobile coverage at all. When the internet is there, it only adds extras, such as accounts and game history. A box that loses contact keeps playing and keeping score, and catches up when the link returns.
Shared core (C11)
The heart of Atalaia: one program, written in the C programming language (its 2011 version, hence C11), that every device running game modes uses. It handles events, scores and radio messages and runs the game modes safely; each kind of hardware only adds a thin layer with the drivers that work its buttons, screen and radio. It exists today.
Lua and game-mode scripts
A game mode is a script: a short text file of rules, such as “hold for 5 seconds to capture”, written in Lua, a small programming language well known from video-game modding. Scripts run in a sandbox, a sealed space with no access to files or the network, so a shared mode cannot harm a device. Organisers never need to touch Lua: they pick a mode and fill in its settings, and a visual, block-based editor will follow in phase 5.
Simulator
A program that plays a game mode with virtual boxes in your web browser: you press the team buttons on screen and watch each box's display and the event log. It runs on an ordinary computer and exists today, so anyone can try Domination and Bomb before any hardware is built.
Field controller
The base station of a game: a small computer, usually a Raspberry Pi or an Android device, that hears every box over the radio, runs the rules that span several boxes, keeps the official score and serves the organisers' console to their phones. If it fails, the boxes keep playing and another capable device takes over, and a single box can run a game without one. It is built (phase 2) and tested on a computer, not yet on a field.
Site server
An optional computer, at the field or rented online, that keeps what lasts beyond one game: player accounts, game history, the chrono registry and the services behind the player app. Games never wait for it. It comes in phase 4, and the community hub plans hosted ones, at cost, for clubs without IT support.

Licences

Open source and free software
The source code, the human-readable recipe of a program, is public, and anyone may use, study, change and share it, even commercially. “Free” here means freedom, not price: fields may charge entry and makers may sell boxes.
Copyleft: GPL and AGPL
The rule that keeps Atalaia open: using it as it is asks nothing of you, but passing on a changed version means sharing its source code under the same licence. The GPL, for the core, firmware, app and modes, applies when you give or sell copies; the AGPL, for the field controller and site server, also applies when you run a changed version for other people over a network. Hardware designs (CERN-OHL-S) and documentation (CC BY-SA) work the same way.

Get involved

Atalaia is built by players, field owners and makers. You can help today by:

  • reviewing the design specification and opening issues;
  • proposing game modes with the game mode idea template;
  • sharing how your field plays, so the defaults fit real games;
  • writing code: start with CONTRIBUTING.md.

Every commit carries a Signed-off-by line, and the project is strictly clean-room, written from scratch: no code, firmware, manuals, sounds or images from any commercial product.

The name

An atalaia is a watchtower: a lookout post on a hill or a stretch of coast. The word came into Portuguese from the Arabic aṭ-ṭalāʾiʿ, "the scouts", and estar de atalaia still means "to be on the lookout".

A field controller sees every prop on the field and tells the GM the moment something changes. Watchtowers were local-first too, and ran the original low-bandwidth mesh: smoke by day, fire by night, relayed from tower to tower. Skirmesh-RF follows the same rules with LoRa.

Field safety

Atalaia props are game equipment. Make them look like game props: bright colours, clear "GAME PROP" labels, and nothing resembling a real explosive device. Transport them in labelled cases, never leave them unattended outside the field, and treat lithium batteries with care.
