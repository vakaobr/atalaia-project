Build guides

Atalaia's props are built from off-the-shelf parts around one ESP32 board, with no custom circuit board. This page has the Field Box's pins and wiring diagrams, its simple output for a siren or a smoke machine, and the guides for the Satellite, the I/O pod, the expansion port and the target addon, shortened; the full guides, with every check and the troubleshooting tables, are in the repository. To put the firmware on a board, download a ready-made release or build it yourself: the firmware guide explains both. The field controller has its own setup guide. The radio modem has its own modem guide.

Not sure what plugs into what? See how the parts talk to each other →

No box has been built from these guides, and the Field Box has no enclosure yet. There is only a bench build of the Field Box (loose wires), playing games. Its pins are checked automatically against the firmware, but every physical step (enclosure, power, runtime, radio range) is untested, and the photos of the other boxes will come with their first builds. If you build one, please tell us what you find.

Safety first

These rules hold for every build on this page, as for the Field Box.

  • Never power a board without its LoRa antenna, not even from USB: transmitting into an open antenna port can destroy the radio's amplifier. Fit both antennas first and leave them on.
  • One Li-ion cell in series only (1S, 3.0–4.2 V): protected cells, a fuse close to the cell and a diode against a cell put in the wrong way round. The board's battery socket and charger take one cell; a 2S pack destroys them. Check the polarity with a meter before every connection: small battery plugs have no standard polarity.
  • Charge under supervision, never in a closed case, only between about 0 and 45 °C, and keep the cells in a fire-safe bag while you work, with the cells out of the box. Never use a cell that is dented, swollen, hot or below 2.5 V.
  • 3.3 V signals only into the board: the LED ring is driven through a level shifter, and every I2C module must pull its lines up to 3.3 V, never 5 V.
  • Never wire GPIO 5 or 46 (the radio's front end; the one exception is the simple output, on the one your board revision leaves free: 46 on a V4.3, 5 on a V4.2) nor GPIO 34 or 38–42 (the board's GPS connector).
  • No game before the transmit-power check of the bring-up list: the firmware keeps the radio within the legal limit with an amplifier gain that has not been measured yet.
  • Plastic, never metal (the radio must get out), weatherproof, in a bright colour with a "GAME PROP" label, and nothing that looks like an explosive device.
  • BBs and paintballs will hit it. Keep the cells and boards away from the walls and out of the line of fire, put a phone's screen behind clear polycarbonate (never glass), and never place a box where players have to shoot at it, unless it is a target.

Field Box

The main prop, with team buttons, a keypad, a colour screen, an LED ring, an NFC reader and a speaker. Its build manual has the parts, the wiring diagrams, power and battery safety, the first flash and troubleshooting; the guides below refer to it for every step they share.

The first build, on the bench

The Field Box now exists off paper, but only as a bench build: the first board to run a whole game, with the screen, buttons and keypad on loose wires and no enclosure yet. It is not a finished product, nor a box built from the guides.

The simple output: a siren, a strobe or a smoke remote

An option for the Field Box and the Satellite, and the preferred way for a box to switch something: a siren, a strobe, a light or a smoke machine's remote, through an off-the-shelf RC relay switch (the kind sold for radio-controlled models). One GPIO sends a servo signal through the level shifter's spare gate: 2.0 ms is on, 1.0 ms is off, in half-second bursts that only the running game renews. If the box hangs, resets or loses power, the pulses stop within 0.5 s and the switch opens its relay. The firmware has it since release 0.7.0, as a build option; it has not been built on hardware yet. The I/O pod's firmware has it too, on GPIO 33 (and 1): see the I/O pod.

  • An RC relay switch that opens its relay when the signal stops: a documented one with a fail-safe on signal loss, or a generic one only after its bench check. One that holds its last state is never used. Its contacts rated for the load.
  • An M8 3-pin socket labelled "OUTPUT 1" (pin 1 not connected, pin 3 ground, pin 4 the signal), a 10 kΩ resistor that holds the pin low while the board starts, a 330 Ω resistor to the socket and a 3-core cable of up to 2 m. It is not the expansion port's M12 socket.
  • The load's own battery, with a fuse to the relay and a 5 V regulator (an RC "BEC") for the switch: the box gives only the signal and ground, never power.
  • A firmware built with the simple output (python tools/docker_fw.py build --simple-out=on). Its pin follows the board revision: GPIO 46 on a V4.3, GPIO 5 on a V4.2. A second output on GPIO 45 (--simple-out=on2) only on a box without the piezo buzzer.
  • Outputs stay off until the field's admin enables them at the box with the admin PIN and gives each its function (siren, strobe, smoke, light, horn) and its longest time on. The box turns them all off at start-up, on a pause and when the game ends (an end-of-game horn may still sound for up to 10 s).

Low-voltage loads only (at most 24 V DC, 2 A recommended), whatever the relay's print says. Never mains, never pyrotechnics; a smoke machine only through a documented low-voltage remote input. Hearing protection near sirens and horns, smoke never in a closed room without the venue's approval, and nothing that looks like an explosive.

Every step and check: section 9.10 of the Field Box's build manual, and each RC switch's bench check before its first game in the bring-up list, step 8b.

Satellite

A small box for a secondary point or a beacon: the Field Box's board with two big team buttons (a third is possible), an LED ring that shows the team holding it, the board's own small screen, and one 18650 cell. About €30–40 in parts (the design's estimate). It runs the Field Box's own firmware with fewer parts fitted: the firmware reports each missing part once at start-up and carries on.

Without a keypad it cannot choose a game on its own: it plays the games a field controller invites it into, as a satellite or the master of Master and Satellites, or as a zone of any mode that needs only team buttons (Domination, Hold-All Domination, Last Stand). It joins a field by USB cable or by the pairing code on its small screen (it has no NFC reader for a field card).

Parts

  • Heltec WiFi LoRa 32 V4 board, revision V4.2 or V4.3, 863–928 MHz, without its shell.
  • An 868 MHz antenna of 2 dBi or less (the board kit's) and a 2.4 GHz antenna, which is required: the first setup, mode downloads and firmware updates use Wi-Fi.
  • Two (or three) 60 mm arcade buttons with 5 V LEDs.
  • A WS2812B LED ring (24 LEDs by default; 12 or 16 with one firmware setting), with the 74AHCT125 level shifter, a 330 Ω resistor and a 1000 µF capacitor, on a small perfboard.
  • A MAX17048 battery gauge, recommended: without it the box never knows its battery level.
  • One protected 18650 cell in a holder (or a 1S pack with a protection board), a 5 A fuse, a Schottky diode, a covered main switch, a 5 V boost converter for the ring, and lever splices: the Field Box's power path with one cell.
  • Sound is optional: a passive piezo buzzer (the cheapest: it beeps the games' sounds as short tone patterns, with a firmware option; not yet built on hardware), the Field Box's MAX98357A amplifier and a small speaker, or both.
  • A simple output is optional: a siren, a strobe, a light or a smoke machine's remote switched by an RC relay switch, as on the Field Box. A master or a Satellite that sounds a siren needs no other box. Not yet built on hardware.
  • A plastic IP65 box in a bright colour, with a vent and cable glands, and "GAME PROP" labels.

Steps

  1. Read the chip next to the board's LoRa antenna connector: KCT8103L is a V4.3 (the firmware's default), GC1109 a V4.2. Fit and label both antennas before the board is ever powered.
  2. Plan the box on paper, then drill every hole before any electronics go in: buttons and ring on the lid, the switch recessed or covered, a vent low down.
  3. Build the power path without the board: cell, fuse, diode, switch, splices; set the boost converter to 5.0–5.2 V on its own before anything is connected to it.
  4. Build the small interconnect board: the 3V3, GND and 5V rails (and SDA and SCL for the gauge) and the level shifter, as in the Field Box's manual.
  5. Wire the buttons (to GND when pressed), the ring through the level shifter, the gauge and the optional amplifier, by the pins above.
  6. If you fit the simple output: the level shifter's spare gate from GPIO 46 (V4.3) or 5 (V4.2), with 10 kΩ to GND, through 330 Ω to the M8 socket's pin 4; the switch powered from the load's battery, never from the box. The GPIO reads 10 kΩ to GND, and nothing joins the socket's pin 4 to 3V3 or 5 V.
  7. Check with the meter, the cell out: no short between the rails, each button only while pressed, the polarity of the board's battery lead.
  8. Build and flash the Field Box firmware as its manual says, set for your board revision and your ring's number of LEDs.
  9. Do the bring-up steps that apply to a Satellite: start-up, buttons, LEDs, battery, and the radio and its transmit power before any game.
  10. Set its name, region and admin PIN in its Wi-Fi setup page from a phone, adopt it into your field by USB cable or pairing code, and invite it into a game from the GM console.

Not decided yet

  • The piezo buzzer of the original design: the firmware drives one (a build option, on GPIO 45), but no buzzer has been built and heard on a real board yet.
  • The enclosure: no box has been chosen yet.
  • The runtime on one cell: not measured.
  • A wiring diagram of its own: for now the Field Box's diagrams show every wire a Satellite has.

I/O pod

The box that lends an Android prop what a phone lacks: big team buttons, a keypad, an LED ring, sound, an NFC reader, a switch that tells when the box is opened, and a LoRa radio. The phone runs the game mode; the pod is its hands and its radio, over a USB cable or Bluetooth. It is the Field Box's hardware without the colour screen, running the pod firmware, which has been tested on a computer but not yet on a board. About €35 in parts (the design's estimate).

Every part but the board is optional: the pod tells the phone what it has, and the phone uses only that. Button 1 is the game's first team, as on a Field Box. An opened enclosure reaches the GM console as a tamper alert.

Simple outputs, optional: a siren, a strobe, a light or a smoke machine's remote through an RC relay switch, wired as the Field Box's simple output but on GPIO 33 (a second one on GPIO 1, with or without the piezo buzzer). A pod has no admin PIN, so building the output in is what enables it, with its use (siren, strobe, smoke, light, horn) and its longest on. The game on the phone switches it with a box's rules, and the pod turns it off by itself: at once when the phone's link goes, and after 1.5 s when the phone stops talking.

The expansion port, optional: the Field Box's M12 socket and port board on the pod's I2C bus (GPIO 17 and 18). A target addon's hits reach the game on the phone while it runs. An output module's relays are lent to the game only by a pod built to lend them (off by default), and the pod turns them off when the phone goes. A pod with every part has little room left to describe itself to the phone: it lends as many outputs as fit, the first ones, and keeps the others off. The outputs and the port are in the pod firmware and tested on a computer only: no pod has switched a real relay or felt a real plate yet.

Parts

  • The Field Box's parts, less the screen: the board, the buttons (up to four), the keypad and its PCF8574 expander, the LED ring with its level shifter, the PN532 NFC reader, the amplifier and speaker, the battery gauge, and the same one-cell power path.
  • Both antennas: LoRa, and 2.4 GHz for Bluetooth.
  • A microswitch that the closed lid holds pressed: the enclosure switch.
  • A panel USB-C socket with data lines (not charge-only) for the phone's cable, and a USB-C OTG cable for the phone.
  • On the bench only: a 3.3 V USB-serial adapter to read the pod's log.
  • Optional, for each simple output: an RC relay switch that opens when its signal stops, a spare gate of the 74AHCT125, 10 kΩ and 330 Ω and an M8 socket, as the Field Box's.
  • Optional: the expansion port's M12 socket and port board, as the Field Box's.

The pod's wiring is the Field Box's without the screen: follow the Field Box's wiring diagrams and leave out the screen and its wires. A drawing of its own does not exist yet.

Steps

  1. Build it as a Field Box without the screen: board revision, antennas, enclosure, power path, interconnect board, then the keypad, ring, NFC reader, buttons and amplifier you fit, each with the manual's checks.
  2. Mount the microswitch so the closed lid presses it, and wire it from GPIO 47 to GND. The pod watches it only after it has read closed once, so a pod without one never raises an alarm.
  3. Build the pod firmware (python tools/docker_fw.py pod; add --simple-out=on for an output, on2 for two, and --module-outputs=on to lend an output module's relays) and flash it from the build-pod folder, as the Field Box's. The boot log lists each output it lends.
  4. Read its log on GPIO 43 with the USB-serial adapter: the USB-C port carries the pod's link to the phone, not the log.
  5. In the app, link it over a USB-C OTG cable, then over Bluetooth (Android asks to pair once).
  6. Run the pod's bring-up checks, including the radio's transmit power before any game.

Not decided yet

  • Outputs and modules on a real pod: the simple outputs and the expansion port are in the pod firmware and tested on a computer, not yet with a real relay, plate or phone; the bench comes next.
  • Power from the phone: on a cable, the phone powers the pod and may charge its cell. Whether it should, and how much a phone gives, are to be checked at the first build.
  • The enclosure, and how the phone and the pod are carried together.
  • Settings: the GM console can push the buttons' colours, the ring's brightness, the volume and whether the enclosure switch reports over the radio; the number of LEDs, the pins and the outputs are set in the firmware build.

Radio modem

The controller's radio: a second Heltec WiFi LoRa 32 V4, flashed with the modem image and plugged into the field controller's USB port. Without it the controller runs and the console opens, but nothing reaches the boxes and nothing is heard from them. It has no Wi-Fi, so it needs only the LoRa antenna.

A modem flashed like this ran with the controller and real boxes on a bench (a computer, the controller in Docker). It has not run on a Raspberry Pi yet, not on macOS, and not on a field.

Steps

  1. Fit the LoRa antenna before the board has any power, also for flashing, and use a USB-C data cable. Decide the radio region: the modem and the boxes must share it.
  2. Download atalaia-modem-<version>-usb.zip and SHA256SUMS from the releases (V4.3, EU868), or build it: python tools/docker_fw.py modem (V4.2, Taiwan, other antennas).
  3. With esptool 4.8.1, erase a new board and write the files of flash_args: esptool --chip esp32s3 -p PORT write_flash "@flash_args", with the port COM5 on Windows, /dev/ttyACM0 on Linux, /dev/cu.usbmodem… on macOS. On Linux, join the dialout group first.
  4. The board's screen shows the tower and MODEM MODE, then LINK waiting. That is a healthy modem that has no controller yet. RADIO FAULT means the radio chip did not start.
  5. Plug it into the controller, find its port (ls /dev/ttyACM*), put it in [modem] port of /etc/atalaia/controller.toml and restart the service (sudo systemctl restart atalaia-controller).
  6. The screen says LINK OK, the console's header says Radio: up, and the controller's log has modem link up.
  7. Switch on an adopted box, create a game and invite the box: it goes sending, invited, joined, and the modem's screen counts packets.

Every command for each system, how to find the port, and a troubleshooting table (charge-only cable, wrong port, port held by another program, dialout, region mismatch, LINK waiting or DOWN, RADIO FAULT, wrong partition table): Set up the radio modem.

Expansion port

Designed, not yet built, and not yet used by the box's firmware. A port can be built now and checked with a meter and the box's own parts; no module answers until the box's port driver runs in its game, with increment 8 of the expansion port plan, still to come.

A waterproof 5-pin M12 socket on the box, where modules plug in from outside: the target addon now, contacts and other sensors later. It is the box's own I2C bus (the keypad's, the NFC reader's, the gauge's and the small screen's) taken out of the enclosure, behind a small port board that keeps a wet, shorted or static-charged cable from taking the box down. It uses no GPIO, so no pin table changes. It is optional on the Field Box, the Satellite and the I/O pod: only a box that carries a target or a sensor needs one. About €6–14 in parts (the design's estimate).

Not for sirens, strobes or smoke: those take the simple output. The port is for inputs, and later for an output module on a box that needs more channels.

Parts

  • A female M12 A-coded 5-pin panel socket (IP67; its contacts are recessed, as pin 1 is live) with a dust cap, and a ready-made male-to-female M12 A-coded 5-pole sensor extension cable of up to 2 m to the module.
  • A TCA4307 hot-swap buffer on a breakout: it joins the port to the box's bus only when both are idle, and cuts it off when a shorted or wet cable holds a line low, so the keypad, the NFC reader, the gauge and the screen carry on.
  • A low-capacitance TVS array (USBLC6-2SC6 class, on an adapter) and two 100 Ω resistors, against static from a hand or a cable at the socket.
  • A 50 mA resettable fuse against a short between pins 1 and 3, and a small Schottky diode (BAT85 class) so that nothing on the cable can feed the box's 3.3 V backwards: the module sees about 3.0 V.
  • Two 2.2 kΩ pull-up resistors on the port's side (modules have none), a small perfboard, and wire to the interconnect board's 3V3, GND, SDA and SCL rails.

The port's pins

The M12 pin-out, with the cable's usual wire colours (check them with a meter). SDA and SCL are the box's I2C bus, behind the buffer.

Modules answer at addresses 0x58–0x5F, clear of every part of the box: input modules from 0x58 (the target), output modules from 0x5C. A box takes at most four, but the port has one socket and no splitter is designed yet, so the guide builds one module per box.

Steps

  1. Drill the socket's hole on the enclosure's side or bottom, away from the antennas and the simple output's socket, where a cable from the ground comes in without a sharp bend, and not facing the shooters. Label it "EXPANSION 3.3 V".
  2. Lay the port board out on a perfboard of about 3 × 4 cm: the box's side at one edge, the socket's at the other, the buffer between them and the TVS next to the socket's header.
  3. Measure the breakout's own pull-up resistors first: with them, the box's bus must still read between 1 and 3.9 kΩ to 3V3, and the port's side about 2.2 kΩ in total.
  4. Wire the supply (the hub's 3V3, the diode, the fuse, the port's 3.3 V), the signals through the buffer, the 100 Ω resistors and the TVS, short ground wires, and the socket's leads in the cable's colours, with pin 5's lead cut short and insulated.
  5. Check with the meter, battery out and USB unplugged: no short between pins 1 and 3, pin 5 open, the diode the right way round, about 100 Ω and 2.2 kΩ where the guide says.
  6. First power, with nothing plugged in: about 3.0 V on pin 1, and the keypad, the NFC reader and the gauge still working, also with the 2 m cable plugged in.
  7. The bench tests you can run now: the rise time at the cable's far end (under 1 µs, with a scope), a shorted SDA with the keypad still working, a short between pins 1 and 3 without a reset, and static at the socket. Please report what you measure in an issue.

Rules for the port

  • 3.3 V only, on every pin, always: no 5 V and no battery voltage ever reach the port, and pin 5 stays not connected.
  • Never power a load from the port: its 25 mA are for a module's processor and sensor. A single WS2812 LED already takes up to 60 mA.
  • Never plug in a bare PCF8574 or MCP23017 relay board, or any I2C board without a processor of its own: its relays would stay on if the box crashed, and it answers where the box looks for its keypad.
  • Cap the socket when nothing is plugged in, and run the cable on the ground, never in the line of fire. Nothing on either side of the port ever touches mains.

Coming with the firmware (increment 8)

  • The box reading the port: the port driver is being written, and the box's game calls it from increment 8. With it come the box's log line for a module, its console's list of modules and the port's step in the bring-up list.
  • The bench tests that need a module: 100 plug and unplug cycles during a game, noise next to a switching siren and strobe, and the module's current.

Not decided yet

  • A splitter for more than one module on a box.
  • A carrier circuit board with the port board on it (perfboard until then), and a wiring diagram of the port, which may follow its first build.

Target addon

Can be built, cannot be used yet. The hardware is decided; its firmware is not written. You can build the plate, the sensor's circuit, the module's enclosure and cable and the box's port now, and check them with a meter; the module cannot be flashed, and no box reads it, until increment 8 of the expansion port plan. No target has been built from the guide yet.

A plate that players shoot at, with a piezo sensor that feels each hit and a small RP2040 board of its own (the target module). It plugs into a box's expansion port and turns the box into a scoring target for Target Windows, whose rules are built and tested in the simulator; the box's own ring of lights shows the window, so the module needs no lights. Any box with a port takes one: a Field Box, a Satellite or an I/O pod.

The piezo, bonded to the back of the plate, turns a hit into a short spike; the module finds its peak, its strength from 0 to 255, and keeps up to 16 hits with their age until the box takes them. The box reads it every 20 ms through the port, about 5 % of the bus, so a hit reaches the game within about 21 ms, and none is counted twice. Game scripts see each hit with its strength and can arm and disarm the target. About €10–15 for the module, plus the plate, the cable and the box's port (the design's estimates).

Parts

  • An RP2040 board, RP2040-Zero class or a Raspberry Pi Pico, run at a reduced clock to stay within the port's 25 mA.
  • A piezo disc of 27–35 mm (and spares: they crack), a 2–3 mm rubber pad and contact adhesive.
  • The sensor's circuit: a 1 MΩ bleed resistor, two divider resistors in sockets (their values are chosen on real plates) and two BAT85-class Schottky diodes that keep the processor's input between ground and 3.3 V.
  • A P-channel MOSFET (AO3401 or SI2301 class) and a 10 kΩ resistor: the module's protection against a hand-made cable wired the wrong way round.
  • Two solder jumpers (or a 2-position DIP switch) for the address: 0x58 with both open, 0x59–0x5B for a second target on the same box.
  • A male M12 A-coded 5-pin panel connector, the M12 extension cable (at most 2 m), a small IP65 enclosure, perfboard and wire.
  • A steel plate or a polycarbonate sheet, and a stand.

Steps

  1. Build and check the box's expansion port first.
  2. The supply: M12 pin 1 through the MOSFET to the RP2040 board's 3V3 pin, never to its 5 V or USB pin; pin 3 to ground; pin 5 cut short and insulated.
  3. Bond the piezo through its pad to the centre of the plate's back; the bleed across its leads, then the divider and the clamps on the way to one of the RP2040's analogue pins (GPIO 26–28).
  4. SDA (pin 2) and SCL (pin 4) to the RP2040's I2C pins, with no pull-up resistors in the module; leave both address jumpers open for 0x58.
  5. The enclosure behind the plate, fixed to the stand rather than to the plate, its connector facing away from the shooters, and the cable running down and along the ground to the box.
  6. Check with the meter, the module unpowered: no short between pins 1 and 3, pin 5 open, no pull-up on pins 2 and 4, the clamps reading as diodes, about 1 MΩ across the piezo.

Coming with the firmware (increment 8)

  • The module's firmware, and its UF2 file from CI and the releases, to flash it by dropping the file onto the drive the board shows over USB.
  • The module's pin table: which RP2040 pins carry I2C, the piezo's input, the address jumpers and its LED, and how the chosen board takes 3.3 V on its 3V3 pin. Until then the guide leaves those wires for later.
  • The box reading the module: its port driver called from the game, each hit passed to the mode's script, and the module in the box's log and the GM console.
  • Measurements at the first build: the module's current (under 25 mA), and the sensor tuned on real steel and polycarbonate plates, which settles the threshold and the divider resistors.
  • Target Windows as a Field Box preset, once the target is bench-tested, and later the I/O pod lending a target to a phone.

Not decided yet

  • The plate: its material grade, size, thickness, stand and angle, from the first builds.
  • A carrier circuit board for the module; perfboard until then.

Safety, whenever anyone shoots at it

  • At the bench: never plug in the module's USB cable and the port cable at the same time, and touch the box's ground before plugging a module in.
  • Eye and face protection for everyone within range while anyone shoots at it, at the bench too.
  • BBs bounce off a hard plate, and some come back: angle a steel plate so they go down to the ground, never flat towards the shooter.
  • Only the plate is a target: never shoot at the box, the cable or the connector, and keep the electronics behind the plate or in their own enclosure.
  • The field's power limits apply to shooting at targets as at players.
